# SCN006: a join that pairs every row with every row

A query joins sources with nothing relating them, or only with a comparison that isn't an equality. BigQuery then pairs every row of one side with every row of the other. The bill doesn't show it: bytes billed stay the same, so a byte limit such as `maximum_bytes_billed` doesn't stop it, while the compute grows with the number of pairs. Counts and sums over the result are multiplied, and without an aggregate BigQuery fails with "Response too large to return".

| Pairs of rows | Severity |
| --- | --- |
| At least 10 billion, with every side's size known | **block** |
| At least 100 million, or a side whose size isn't known | **warn** |
| Any number, under a `LIMIT` that lets BigQuery stop early | **warn** at most |
| Fewer, with every side's size known or bounded | not reported |

The pair count is the product of each side's estimated rows. A table's rows are its row count times the share of its bytes in the partitions its filters keep. Both thresholds are policy settings (`cross_join_warn_pairs`, `cross_join_block_pairs`).

A side's size isn't known when something other than whole partitions can change it:

- a filter that isn't a partition filter, or one finer than a partition, such as a five-second window in a daily partition;
- a correlated subquery, `INTERSECT`, or a reader's filter that can't move down to the table;
- `TABLESAMPLE`, or an `UNNEST` of the table's own array, which repeats or drops its rows;
- a join inside the side, since a key that isn't unique can give more rows than either table;
- a CTE or subquery that isn't a single row.

Then the finding warns but never blocks, because that side may hold a handful of rows. A comparison between the sides, as in `o.created_at < u.created_at`, filters pairs, not either side's rows, so it doesn't count.

A `LIMIT` on the SELECT with no `ORDER BY`, `GROUP BY`, `DISTINCT`, aggregate or window function lets BigQuery stop once it has that many rows. An `OFFSET` doesn't: BigQuery makes the pairs it skips too, so `LIMIT 10 OFFSET 1000000000` counts as no `LIMIT`. Measured, `users CROSS JOIN orders LIMIT 10` took 0.14 slot-seconds. The finding then warns that the rows are arbitrary pairs, and never blocks.

## Evidence

Measured on 2026-10-03 on `thelook_ecommerce`, where `users` has 100,000 rows and `orders` 125,417 ([#23](https://github.com/tjslezak/Scanisaur/issues/23)). Every query billed 20 to 30 MiB.

| Query | Answer | Slot time | Wall time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `users JOIN orders ON o.user_id = u.id` | 125,417 | 0.06 s | 0.4 s |
| `FROM users u, orders o WHERE o.status = 'Complete'`: the key forgotten | **3,130,400,000** | 1 s | 0.4 s |
| `users CROSS JOIN orders`, a sum over the 12.5 billion pairs | a number | **160 s** | 1.6 s |
| `users JOIN orders ON o.created_at < u.created_at`: no equality | 3.3 billion | **155 s** | 1.6 s |
| `ON o.user_id = u.id OR o.order_id = u.id`: an `OR` of equalities | 225,000 | 0.7 s | 0.6 s |
| Three tables, the first join with no condition of its own, all related in `WHERE` | 181,780 | 0.1 s | 0.2 s |
| `events CROSS JOIN users`: 243 billion pairs | a number | **2,363 s** | 3.1 s |
| `users CROSS JOIN orders`, every pair as a row | "Response too large to return" | | |
| The same with `LIMIT 10` | 10 arbitrary pairs | 0.14 s | |
| Trends × Wikipedia, one day each (dry run) | about 10^13 rows | | 1.7 GB, about a cent |

Slot time grows by about 10 seconds per billion pairs. On-demand projects share that compute with their other queries; under capacity (Editions) pricing, slot time is what is billed. BigQuery stopped none of these queries.

## When it applies

Within each SELECT, the sources in `FROM` and `JOIN`, whether tables, CTEs or subqueries, fall into groups by the equalities in `WHERE`, `ON` and `USING` that relate them, including the `ON` of a join in parentheses. An equality relates two sources when each side reads exactly one of them. With two on one side, as `a.x + b.y = c.z`, BigQuery must pair `a` with `b` first. When there is more than one group, SCN006 applies, placed at the join that brings in the second group. Each SELECT gets its own finding, so two `UNION` branches alike are reported twice.

- **No condition:** a comma join or `CROSS JOIN` with no key in `WHERE`, `ON TRUE`, or an `ON` that filters one side only, as `ON o.status = 'Complete'`. Outer joins count the same way.
- **Only comparisons that aren't equalities,** as `ON o.created_at < u.created_at` or `BETWEEN`: BigQuery compares every pair. The fix suggests an equality alongside the comparison.
- **A group that is itself a join,** as a third table joined to neither of two keyed ones. Its size isn't known, so the finding warns.

## When it stays silent

- **Equalities, wherever they are written:** BigQuery reorders joins, so a condition in `WHERE` that relates the first two tables through a third connects them. An `OR` of equalities counts as an equality, and so does an `IN` list of columns, as `o.user_id IN (u.id, u.alt_id)`.
- **A correlated subquery whose sources each relate to the outer row,** as `(SELECT COUNT(*) FROM o, i WHERE o.user_id = u.id AND i.user_id = u.id)`.
- **`UNNEST`:** of a joined row's array, as `FROM t, UNNEST(t.items)`, which belongs to that row, through further `UNNEST`s too; or of a literal or generated array, such as `GENERATE_DATE_ARRAY`.
- **A source with at most one row:** an aggregate without `GROUP BY`, `LIMIT 1`, or a SELECT without `FROM`, as `CROSS JOIN (SELECT CURRENT_DATE() AS today)`.
- **Small products:** fewer pairs than the warn threshold, as a table of 180,000 rows with a 365-day calendar, a CTE with `LIMIT 10`, or an empty table. A filter only shrinks a side, so its unfiltered size bounds the product.
- **`HAVING` and `QUALIFY`** don't connect sources: they run after the join, on the pairs.
- **Subqueries in conditions,** such as `EXISTS` and `IN (SELECT …)`, which aren't joins.
- **An outer `LIMIT 0`,** which reads nothing.

## Fix

Add the condition that relates the sources. When the column names point to one, the fix names it as an example: a `<table>_id` column on one side and `id` on the other, as `o.user_id = u.id`, or a shared `*_id` column. If every pair is intended, keep one side small, such as a single-row subquery or a short list of dates.
